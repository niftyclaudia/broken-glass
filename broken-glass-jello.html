Broken Glass is a study of the potluck classic: cubes of cherry, lime, orange and berry-blue jello set in a creamy condensed-milk layer on a graham-cracker crust. Grab it and it wobbles. Slice it and every cut face becomes a new mosaic. Stamp it with a Jiggler cutter and lift the shape right out.

Inside the experiment +

Physics. The cake is a soft body made of small tetrahedra. Each step, every tetrahedron is pulled back toward its resting shape, rotated to match how it has turned, so the jello wobbles instead of stretching like rubber. The crust is stiffer than the cream, and the cubes ride along inside.

Cutting. Every piece is stored as a flat distance field (a grid of “how far am I from the edge” values). A knife stroke becomes a vertical plane; each piece it crosses is split along that plane in its own resting frame, the corners are softened, and each separate part becomes a new piece that inherits the old one’s motion.

Jiggler cutters. Each cutter is a rounded outline that uses the same distance field, so the ring and the hole it leaves always match. Stamping splits a flat piece into the part inside the wall and the part outside it.

Rendering. Each pixel of jello marches a ray through the cake’s resting shape: the cream scatters light like milk, the cubes absorb colour like stained glass, and whatever sits behind shows through, bent a little.

The dessert. Broken glass cake folds cubes of several jello flavours into a creamy base. It’s also called stained glass, crown jewel or cathedral window dessert, and gelatina mosaico in Mexico. It dates from 1950s Jell-O recipes and was a potluck staple for decades.

Illustrative scale: the simulation isn’t tuned to real gelatin.
